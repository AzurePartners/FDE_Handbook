**In one sentence:** Good discovery uses open-ended questions and the "5 Whys" to get from symptom to root cause, and it carefully separates facts from opinions from incentives, never treating the customer's first statement as the requirement.

## What it is

Two disciplines. **5 Whys / laddering:** ask "why" (or "what would that let you do?") repeatedly to move from a surface request to the underlying goal. **Open-ended questions** ("walk me through the last time…") elicit real detail, unlike leading yes/no questions. And you continually sort what you hear into **facts** (verifiable: "we process 2,000 tickets a week"), **opinions** ("I think users want X"), and **incentives** (what the speaker is motivated to say, e.g. a team wanting to justify headcount). The first statement a customer makes is almost never the actual requirement; it's a starting point to probe.

## Why an FDE needs this

Take the first statement at face value and you build the wrong thing. Confuse an opinion for a fact and you design on sand. Miss an incentive and you're steered by politics you didn't see. These techniques are how an FDE extracts a reliable problem definition from messy human input, the core skill of discovery.

## Key concepts

- **5 Whys / laddering:** repeat "why" to reach the root goal.
- **Open-ended questions:** ask for stories and specifics, not yes/no or hypotheticals.
- **Facts vs opinions:** verify claims; label unverified beliefs as opinions.
- **Incentives:** notice what each person is motivated to say.
- **First statement ≠ requirement:** treat it as a probe-point, not the spec.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Whatever the customer states is the requirement."** It's a starting point; the real requirement is usually a few "whys" deeper.
- **"If they said it, it's a fact."** Distinguish verifiable facts from opinions and incentive-driven claims.
- **"Leading questions get answers faster."** They bias the answer; open questions surface the truth.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>How do you get from a stated request to the root problem?</summary>

I ladder up with "why" and "what would that let you do?" until I reach the underlying goal, and I ask open-ended, story-based questions ("walk me through the last time this happened") rather than leading ones. Throughout, I separate facts from opinions and notice incentives, so I don't treat the first statement as the requirement.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why separate facts, opinions, and incentives?</summary>

Because designing on an opinion mistaken for a fact, or being steered by an unseen incentive, leads to the wrong solution. Verifiable facts anchor the problem; opinions are hypotheses to test; and knowing each speaker's incentives lets me weigh their input and spot political dynamics shaping what I'm told.

</details>
