**In one sentence:** FDE work is measured by the outcome delivered, not the hours billed, which is why the role is not simply "on-site coding", you own that the customer's problem gets solved, not just that you showed up and wrote code.

## What it is

Two ways to frame responsibility. **Time-and-materials (T&M):** you're paid for hours/effort; success is "I did the work I was asked to." **Outcome ownership:** success is "the customer's problem is actually solved", the working, adopted result. FDE work leans toward outcome ownership: it's not enough to have coded what was requested if it didn't solve the real problem or never got adopted. This is why "FDE is just on-site coding" misses the point, the coding is a means; the owned deliverable is the outcome.

## Why an FDE needs this

The mindset changes what you do. If you own the outcome, you push back on a request that won't solve the real problem (rather than just building it), you care about adoption and handoff (not just shipping), and you measure success by impact (not effort). Interviewers and customers probe for this, an FDE who thinks in outcomes is trusted with ambiguity; one who thinks in tickets needs everything spec'd.

## Key concepts

- **T&M mindset:** paid for effort; success = work done as asked.
- **Outcome mindset:** success = problem solved and adopted.
- **Implication for discovery:** you validate the request serves the real problem.
- **Implication for delivery:** adoption, handoff, and impact matter, not just shipping.
- **Not "on-site coding":** the code is a means to an owned outcome.

## Common misconceptions

- **"I built what they asked, so I succeeded."** If it didn't solve the real problem or wasn't adopted, the outcome failed.
- **"FDE = a contractor writing code on-site."** The differentiator is owning the outcome, discovery through adoption.
- **"Owning outcomes means over-promising."** It means being honest about what will actually move the outcome, including saying no.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>Why is FDE work described as outcome ownership rather than time-and-materials?</summary>

Because success is defined by whether the customer's problem is actually solved and adopted, not by hours billed or code written. That's why it's more than on-site coding: an FDE validates that the work serves the real problem, cares about adoption and handoff, and measures impact, owning the result, not just the effort.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does an outcome mindset change how you handle a questionable request?</summary>

Instead of just building it (T&M thinking), I check whether it actually serves the real problem, and if not, I push back with the trade-off and a better alternative. Owning the outcome means I'm responsible for the result, so I won't quietly build something that won't move it.

</details>
