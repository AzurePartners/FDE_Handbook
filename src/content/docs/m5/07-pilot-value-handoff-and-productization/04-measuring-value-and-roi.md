**In one sentence:** ROI translates the solution's impact into business terms, time and cost savings, revenue gains, and risk reduction, while being honest about the strength of the evidence and what can't be attributed to the solution.

## What it is

**ROI (return on investment)** frames value the way the business does: **time/cost savings** (hours or dollars reduced), **revenue gains** (sales or throughput increased), and **risk reduction** (fewer errors, better compliance, avoided losses). Measuring it well means quantifying these against the baseline, and being explicit about **evidence strength** (a controlled before/after is strong; a vibe is weak) and **attribution** (what the solution caused versus other factors). It's the business-language expression of the value metrics, the case that justifies the investment and the next phase.

## Why an FDE needs this

Customers justify spend in ROI terms, and demonstrated ROI is what closes an engagement credibly and earns expansion. But over-claiming ROI you can't defend backfires, when finance scrutinizes it and the numbers don't hold, you lose trust. An FDE has to build an honest ROI case: quantify the real savings/gains/risk reduction, state how strong the evidence is, and acknowledge what can't be attributed. That honesty is more persuasive and durable than an inflated number.

## Key concepts

- **Three value types:** time/cost savings, revenue gains, risk reduction.
- **Quantify vs baseline:** measure the change against the pre-project state.
- **Evidence strength:** be clear how solid the measurement is (controlled vs anecdotal).
- **Attribution:** separate the solution's effect from other causes; don't claim all of it.
- **Honest > inflated:** a defensible modest number beats an indefensible big one.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Bigger ROI numbers are better."** Only if defensible; inflated ROI collapses under scrutiny and destroys trust.
- **"ROI is just cost savings."** It also includes revenue gains and risk reduction, which are often the bigger value.
- **"Report the number, not the caveats."** Stating evidence strength and attribution limits is what makes the ROI credible.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>How do you build an ROI case for a solution?</summary>

Quantify the value in business terms against the baseline, time and cost savings, revenue gains, and risk reduction, and pair each figure with how strong the evidence is and an honest attribution of what the solution caused versus other factors. A defensible, caveated ROI case is more persuasive and durable than an inflated number that collapses under finance's scrutiny.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why be honest about attribution and evidence strength in ROI?</summary>

Because ROI gets scrutinized, especially by finance, and an over-claimed number that doesn't hold up destroys credibility and jeopardizes expansion. Being explicit that, say, part of the improvement may owe to other factors, and how solid the measurement is, makes the case trustworthy, which is what actually justifies further investment.

</details>
