**In one sentence:** Scoping turns discovery into an agreed plan, the problem statement, target user, MVP, must-haves vs nice-to-haves, explicit non-goals, and dependencies, so everyone commits to the same thing and the smallest valuable version ships first.

## What it is

**Scope** defines the boundaries of the work. Its parts: a crisp **problem statement**; the **target user**; the **MVP** (smallest version delivering real value); a split of features into **must-have** (in the MVP) and **nice-to-have** (later); explicit **non-goals** (what you're deliberately not doing); and **dependencies** (what the work needs, access, data, other teams). The non-goals and the must/nice split are the parts that actually control scope, they make the hard exclusion decisions explicit instead of assumed.

## Why an FDE needs this

Verbal scope drifts and everyone remembers it differently. A written scope, especially the non-goals, is the anchor you point to when someone asks for something extra, and it forces the uncomfortable but essential decision of what's *out*. Defining the MVP keeps a time-boxed engagement shipping something real rather than everything half-built. It's the bridge from understanding the problem to building the right slice.

## Key concepts

- **Problem statement & target user:** who has the problem and what it is.
- **MVP:** smallest slice delivering real, measurable value.
- **Must-have vs nice-to-have:** MVP is must-haves only.
- **Non-goals:** explicit exclusions; the strongest anti-scope-creep tool.
- **Dependencies:** access, data, teams the work relies on.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Scope is the feature list."** It's also the MVP, the non-goals, and the dependencies, the exclusions matter most.
- **"We discussed it, no need to write it."** Verbal scope drifts; written-and-agreed is the anchor.
- **"MVP means low quality."** It means minimal scope done well, one complete valuable path, not everything half-built.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>What goes into scoping a project?</summary>

A problem statement, the target user, the MVP (smallest valuable slice), a must-have vs nice-to-have split, explicit non-goals, and dependencies, written down and agreed. The non-goals and the must/nice split are what actually control scope by making the exclusion decisions explicit.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why are non-goals so important?</summary>

Because scope creep enters through things never explicitly excluded. Stating what you're deliberately not doing sets expectations, prevents "I assumed you'd also build X," and gives you a clear reference when new requests arrive during the build.

</details>
