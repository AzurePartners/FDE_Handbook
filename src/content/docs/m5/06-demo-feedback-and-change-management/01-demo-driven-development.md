**In one sentence:** Demo-driven development means showing a narrow, working, end-to-end path on a regular cadence rather than accumulating features for one big final reveal, so misunderstandings surface early while they're cheap to fix.

## What it is

Instead of building in silence and unveiling everything at the end, you deliver and **demo a thin, complete slice** frequently (often weekly), then the next slice, and the next. Each demo shows a real end-to-end path working (however narrow), not slides or mockups. The cadence creates a tight feedback loop: every demo is a chance to confirm you're building the right thing, or catch that you aren't, before more is built on the misunderstanding. It's the same idea as iterative delivery, framed around the demo as the forcing function.

## Why an FDE needs this

The longer you go without showing the customer, the wider the gap can grow between what you built and what they meant, and the more expensive it is to correct. Frequent demos keep that gap small, keep the customer engaged (silence reads as trouble), and surface new requirements that only become clear once people see something real. Going dark for weeks and doing a big reveal is how FDE projects end in a painful "that's not what we wanted."

## Key concepts

- **Thin end-to-end slice:** a complete narrow path, not half of every feature.
- **Regular cadence:** demo often (e.g. weekly), not once at the end.
- **Real working software:** demo the thing, not slides; working software surfaces truth.
- **Feedback loop:** each demo confirms or corrects direction cheaply.
- **Silence is risk:** frequent contact maintains trust and alignment.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Demo when it's polished."** Waiting for polish delays feedback; show rough-but-working slices early.
- **"A demo is a performance."** It's a feedback session; the goal is to learn, not just impress.
- **"Progress = features built."** Progress = working slices the customer has seen and validated.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>What is demo-driven development and why use it?</summary>

Delivering and demoing a narrow, working, end-to-end slice on a regular cadence instead of accumulating features for one final reveal. It's used because the gap between what you built and what the customer meant grows with silence and is cheapest to fix early; frequent demos keep it small, keep the customer engaged, and surface new requirements once they see something real.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why demo a thin end-to-end slice rather than one complete feature?</summary>

Because an end-to-end slice proves the whole path works and integrates, which is where the real risks and misunderstandings hide, while a single fully-built feature can still fail to connect to the rest. A thin complete path gives the most learning per demo and something real to react to.

</details>
