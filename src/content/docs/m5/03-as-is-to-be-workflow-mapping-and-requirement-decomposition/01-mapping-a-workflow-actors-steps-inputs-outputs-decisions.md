**In one sentence:** Mapping a workflow means breaking a customer's process into its actors, steps, inputs, outputs, decision points, pain points, and handoffs, so you see exactly how work actually flows before deciding what to change.

## What it is

A **workflow map** is a structured breakdown of how a task really gets done:

- **Actors:** who (or what system) performs each part.
- **Steps:** the concrete actions in sequence.
- **Inputs/outputs:** what each step consumes and produces.
- **Decision points:** where the path branches on a condition.
- **Pain points:** where it's slow, error-prone, or frustrating.
- **Handoffs:** where work passes between people/systems (a frequent source of delay and error).

Mapping it makes the invisible visible, especially the handoffs and decision points where problems concentrate.

## Why an FDE needs this

You can't improve a process you haven't seen clearly, and customers describe their workflow in vague, idealized terms. A concrete map surfaces the real steps, the branching, and especially the handoffs and pain points, which is where automation or AI can actually help. It's the bridge from discovery (the problem) to design (what to build), and it grounds scope in reality.

## Key concepts

- **Actors:** human and system participants.
- **Steps & sequence:** the actual path, not the idealized one.
- **Inputs/outputs per step:** what flows through.
- **Decision points:** conditional branches.
- **Pain points:** slow/error-prone spots, targets for improvement.
- **Handoffs:** cross-actor transitions; common failure/delay points.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The described process is the real one."** The real workflow has undocumented steps, branches, and workarounds; map what actually happens.
- **"Steps are what matter."** Handoffs and decision points are where most delay and error live; capture them explicitly.
- **"Mapping is busywork."** It's what reveals where a solution can actually help and grounds scope.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>What do you capture when mapping a customer workflow?</summary>

Actors (who/what does each part), the concrete steps in sequence, the inputs and outputs of each, the decision points where it branches, the pain points where it's slow or error-prone, and the handoffs where work passes between people or systems. Handoffs and decision points get special attention because that's where problems concentrate.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why focus on handoffs?</summary>

Because handoffs, work passing between people or systems, are where delays, dropped context, and errors most often occur. Making them explicit on the map shows where the process actually breaks down and where automation or better tooling can have the biggest effect.

</details>
