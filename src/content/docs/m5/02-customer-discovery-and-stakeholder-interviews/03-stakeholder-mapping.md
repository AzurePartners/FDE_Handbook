**In one sentence:** Stakeholder mapping identifies everyone who affects or is affected by the project, decision makers, users, technical owners, data owners, security/legal, and skeptics, so you know whose buy-in you need, whose access you depend on, and whose concerns could block you.

## What it is

A project has many **stakeholders**, and they aren't interchangeable:

- **Decision makers:** approve, fund, and prioritize.
- **Users:** actually use the thing daily.
- **Technical owners:** own the systems you'll build on/integrate with.
- **Data owners:** control access to the data you need.
- **Security/legal:** gate what's allowed (review, compliance).
- **Skeptics:** may resist or block; often surface real risks.

**Mapping** them means listing who's who and understanding each one's interest, influence, and concerns, so you engage the right people the right way.

## Why an FDE needs this

The person who requests the project rarely determines its success alone. Talk only to the sponsor and you can build something users reject, hit a data owner who won't grant access, fail a security review you didn't anticipate, or get blocked by a skeptic. Mapping stakeholders early tells you whose buy-in and access you need and whose concerns to address before they become blockers.

## Key concepts

- **Interest vs influence:** who cares, and who has power over the outcome; manage each accordingly.
- **Users ≠ buyers:** the person paying often isn't the person using; talk to both.
- **Data/technical owners:** your access and integration depend on them; engage early.
- **Security/legal:** involve early so review isn't a late surprise.
- **Skeptics:** engage them; their objections are risk signals and their buy-in aids adoption.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The sponsor speaks for everyone."** Sponsors don't do the daily work or own the data; map and engage the others.
- **"Skeptics are obstacles to route around."** They surface real risks and their buy-in often decides adoption.
- **"Security/legal can come at the end."** Late involvement means late blockers; engage them early.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>Who do you map as stakeholders, and why not just the requester?</summary>

Decision makers, users, technical owners, data owners, security/legal, and skeptics. Not just the requester because success depends on more than them: users must adopt it, data and technical owners control access, security/legal gate what's allowed, and skeptics can block it. Mapping them tells me whose buy-in and access I need and whose concerns to address early.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you handle a known skeptic?</summary>

Engage them directly and early rather than avoiding them. Their objections usually point to real risks worth addressing, and winning even partial buy-in helps adoption. Ignoring a skeptic until launch is how a project gets blocked at the finish line.

</details>
