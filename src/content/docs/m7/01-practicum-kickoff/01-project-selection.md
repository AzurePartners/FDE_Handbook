**In one sentence:** Project selection is choosing one realistic customer problem, from an approved domain, that you can take from discovery to handoff, and getting a Problem Brief signed off before you build anything.

## What it is

The practicum asks you to deliver one project end to end. Before any design work, you pick the project. The approved domains are Finance, Healthcare, Content, Education, or another business domain your reviewer approves.

Most learners adapt an M6 archetype; an original project in an approved domain is allowed if the reviewer approves its Problem Brief. For example, the Tutor / Support RAG archetype becomes the Course Support Assistant for a training provider that answers student questions about schedules, refunds and course material. The M6 case overviews show what each archetype looks like when finished.

The output is a short Problem Brief that a reviewer approves.

## Why an FDE needs this

Real engagements often fail at the start. A team picks the exciting problem instead of the deliverable one, then discovers in week three that the data is locked or no one owns the process. A good FDE chooses a project where the customer, the data and the success test all exist.

A project that needs live bank APIs or patient records you cannot access will stall at Alpha.

Compare the four archetypes before choosing. ### What you produce

An approved Problem Brief, one page:

- Customer and domain (example: a training provider, Education)
- Archetype adapted, or "original" (example: Tutor / Support RAG)
- Problem in one sentence, in the customer's words
- Primary user and what they do today
- Data you can actually get (sample course FAQ, policy documents, past tickets)
- One-line success test (example: "agents stop answering the same 20 questions by hand")

### Pass bar

A reviewer approves the brief when all of these are observable:

- The problem names a real user and a real current workflow, not a technology.
- You can name where the data comes from and show a sample file.
- The project fits the time you have, with one narrow MVP in sight.
- The customer organization may be fictional but realistic; the stakeholders or qualified proxies you will interview are real people you can reach.
- The problem statement contains no solution words such as "chatbot".

Filter: data in hand, users reachable, recoverable failures, one workflow.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Pick the most impressive project."** Impressive projects stall. Reviewers score a complete, tested delivery over an ambitious unfinished one.
- **"The brief describes the solution."** It describes the problem. Solutions come after discovery and validation.
- **"I can fix a bad pick later."** Switching after Alpha starts costs most of your time. The [Go / No-Go Decision](./04-go-no-go-decision.md) is the planned exit, not a rescue.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>Walk me through how you chose your project.</summary>

I started from the Tutor / Support RAG archetype and tested it against a filter: data in hand, reachable users, recoverable failures and one narrow workflow. The Course Support Assistant passed because I had sample FAQs and refund policies, and a wrong answer means a support agent corrects it. I wrote that into a one-page Problem Brief and got it approved.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What did you reject, and why?</summary>

I rejected a trading-style project because live market data and compliance review were out of reach in the time available. I kept the lesson that data access, not model quality, decides what is deliverable.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do you know a problem is worth solving before you build?</summary>

I check that a real user performs the task today, that it costs measurable time or errors, and that someone cares enough to adopt a fix. Validation is a separate step, covered in [Project Validation](./03-project-validation.md).

</details>
