**In one sentence:** Behavioral rounds and project deep dives are the same skill from two directions: STAR gives a 60–90-second story a shape (Situation, Task, Action, Result), and the FDE version adds judgment, trade-offs, failure, and retrospective so the interviewer hears how you decide, not just what happened.

## What it is

The behavioral or values round asks for stories: owning something end to end, a demanding stakeholder, a bad decision reversed, alignment without authority, shipping with imperfect information, a failure, a pattern spotted across customers, a time you said no. The project deep dive takes one item from your resume and spends 45 minutes on it, following whatever thread the interviewer pulls. Both are scored on ownership language, specificity, and honesty about what went wrong. The FDE adaptation of STAR adds three beats: the **judgment** you made and the alternative you rejected, the **failure** or setback inside the story, and the **retrospective** — what you would do differently.

## Why an FDE needs this

Behavioral questions appear at every stage of FDE loops, including recruiter screens and the start of technical rounds; some startups have no dedicated round and ask them inside the case. The stories are the interviewer's only evidence of how you behave with a customer when nobody from the company is watching. A story with no failure and no trade-off is read as either rehearsed or not yours. Deep dives additionally check whether the resume was accurate; every bullet is a potential 45 minutes.

## Key concepts

### The FDE story shape

| Beat | Time | What to say |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | 10 s | The customer, the constraint, the baseline |
| Task | 10 s | What you owned, specifically |
| Judgment | 15 s | The decision, the alternative you rejected, why |
| Action | 20 s | What you did, in "I" statements |
| Failure | 10 s | What went wrong or nearly did |
| Result | 15 s | The number, with evidence strength; what changed for the customer |
| Retrospective | 10 s | What you would do differently |

Spoken, that is about 90 seconds. Time each story until it lands inside the window; interviewers will ask follow-ups, so leave room.

### Eight stories to prepare

End-to-end ownership; a difficult stakeholder; a reversed technical decision; alignment without authority; shipping under a deadline with imperfect information; a failure and what you learned; a pattern spotted across customers or users that changed how the team worked; saying no to a customer and holding the line. Map each to a portfolio artifact where possible.

### Deep-dive readiness

For the capstone, be able to go three levels down on any component: why this retrieval approach, what the eval showed, which failure case drove the change, what the trace looked like. Interviewers pull threads until they find the edge; the edge should be a real unknown, not a gap in your memory.

### "I" versus "we"

Say "I" for your decisions and actions and "we" for the team's. Interviewers screen hard for candidates who can name their own contribution; a story told entirely in "we" is scored as participation, not ownership.

### Company values

AI labs score mission alignment inside behavioral rounds. Read the company's published values and recent work, and make sure at least two stories connect to them without forcing it.

## Common misconceptions

- **"STAR is enough."** Plain STAR produces a tidy story with no judgment in it. Add the decision, the failure, and the retrospective.
- **"Longer stories show depth."** Depth comes from follow-ups. A four-minute answer removes the interviewer's chance to ask them.
- **"Failure stories should end well."** They should end honestly. A failure with a clear lesson and a changed behavior is stronger than one rescued by luck.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>Tell me about a time you disagreed with a customer and held the line.</summary>

Situation and what they wanted; what they were right about; the principle you held (governance, a written non-goal, safety); the option you offered instead; the outcome and the relationship afterward; what you would do earlier next time.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Walk me through the most technically challenging thing you shipped.</summary>

Lead with the customer problem, not the technology. Then the judgment (the approach chosen and the one rejected), the hardest part, the failure along the way, the measured result, and the retrospective. Expect the interviewer to go three levels deeper on one component.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Tell me about a time a deployment went badly.</summary>

Own the whole outcome, including parts you did not build. Cause, what you did in the first hour, what you told the customer and when, the fix, and the process change afterward.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is a technical decision you reversed?</summary>

See [Technical Debt and Architecture Change Stories](../02-coding-debugging-system-design/04-technical-debt-and-architecture-change-stories.md). "I have not had to reverse one" is a weak answer.

</details>
