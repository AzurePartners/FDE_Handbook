**In one sentence:** A PRD (Product Requirements Document) describes *what* to build and why; an SOW (Statement of Work) is a contractual document defining project scope, mutual responsibilities, milestones, acceptance evidence, and how changes are handled, and an FDE engagement often needs both.

## What it is

A **PRD** is product-facing: the problem, users, requirements, and success criteria, what should be built and why. An **SOW** is contract-facing: it defines the **project scope**, each party's **responsibilities**, the **milestones** and timeline, the **acceptance evidence** (what proves a deliverable is done), and the **change mechanism** (how new requests are handled and priced). The PRD guides the building; the SOW governs the commercial relationship and protects both sides by making obligations and change-handling explicit.

## Why an FDE needs this

FDE work is delivery under an agreement, so you'll encounter or contribute to both. The PRD keeps the build aligned to the real requirements; the SOW is what prevents disputes, it pins down who's responsible for what, what "done" means (acceptance evidence), and crucially how changes are handled, so scope changes become a defined process, not an argument. Understanding the distinction lets you write requirements in the PRD and flag scope/commercial matters that belong in the SOW.

## Key concepts

|  | PRD | SOW |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Answers | What to build & why | Contractual scope & terms |
| Contains | Problem, users, requirements, success | Scope, responsibilities, milestones, acceptance, change process |
| Purpose | Guide the build | Govern the engagement, protect both sides |

- **Acceptance evidence (SOW):** what proves a deliverable is complete.
- **Change mechanism (SOW):** the defined path for new requests, key to scope control.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A PRD and SOW are the same doc."** One is product requirements, the other a contractual scope agreement; they serve different purposes.
- **"The SOW is just legal boilerplate."** Its scope, acceptance, and change-mechanism clauses directly shape delivery and prevent disputes.
- **"You don't need acceptance criteria in writing."** Undefined "done" is where delivery disputes start; the SOW pins it down.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>What's the difference between a PRD and an SOW?</summary>

A PRD is product-facing, the problem, users, requirements, and success criteria, guiding what gets built and why. An SOW is contractual, defining project scope, each party's responsibilities, milestones, acceptance evidence, and how changes are handled and priced. The PRD guides the build; the SOW governs the engagement and protects both sides.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does the SOW's change mechanism matter to an FDE?</summary>

Because scope changes are inevitable, and without a defined change mechanism each one becomes an argument about whether it's included. A clear mechanism turns new requests into a recorded process, clarification vs formal change vs future phase, with agreed impact on scope, timeline, and cost, which protects both the relationship and the delivery.

</details>
