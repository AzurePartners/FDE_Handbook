**In one sentence:** Prove value against a baseline measured before the project, using success metrics tied to the business outcome and a North Star metric that captures overall value, and be honest about attribution, don't treat model metrics as if they were direct business value.

## What it is

**Baseline:** the pre-project measurement of the current state, without it, you can't show change. **Success metrics:** specific, measurable outcomes tied to the business goal from discovery (resolution time, manual hours, conversion). **North Star:** the single metric that best captures the value delivered, that the team aligns on. **Attribution:** the honest accounting of how much of an observed change your solution actually caused, versus other factors (seasonality, a parallel initiative). And the key discipline: a **model metric** (accuracy, F1) is not business value, 92% accuracy means nothing if resolution time didn't improve.

## Why an FDE needs this

Value has to be proven, not asserted, and "I think it helped" is weak ground. A baseline plus outcome metrics is what lets the customer justify the investment and lets you show real impact. Confusing model metrics for business value is a classic trap that makes a technically successful project look valuable when it delivered nothing the business cares about. Honest attribution protects your credibility, over-claiming impact you can't defend backfires when scrutinized.

## Key concepts

- **Baseline first:** measure current state before building; no baseline, no proof of change.
- **Outcome metrics, not vanity:** tied to the business goal (time, cost, revenue), not model stats.
- **North Star:** the one metric that best represents overall value.
- **Attribution honesty:** separate your impact from other causes; don't over-claim.
- **Model metric ≠ business value:** accuracy is an input to value, not value itself.

## Common misconceptions

- **"We'll know if it helped."** Without a baseline and defined metrics, "helped" is opinion; measure against the pre-project state.
- **"High model accuracy = success."** It's an output; if the business outcome didn't move, the project didn't deliver value.
- **"All the improvement is ours."** Be honest about attribution; other factors may share credit, and over-claiming erodes trust.

## Typical interview questions

<details>
<summary>How do you prove the value of a delivered solution?</summary>

Measure a baseline before the project, then compare against success metrics tied to the business outcome (like resolution time or manual hours), ideally rolled up into a North Star metric. I report it honestly, including where it fell short, and I'm careful about attribution, separating what the solution caused from other factors, rather than treating model accuracy as if it were business value.

</details>

<details>
<summary>A model is 92% accurate but the customer says it didn't help. How is that possible?</summary>

Because accuracy is a model metric, not a business outcome. If the goal was to cut handling time or manual work and that didn't move, maybe the 8% errors are the costly cases, or the workflow didn't change, the project failed on the metric that mattered. Value is measured by the business outcome against a baseline, not by model stats.

</details>
